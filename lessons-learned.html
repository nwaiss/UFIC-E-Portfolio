Lessons Learned

Even though my initial intention was to improve my logical and coding abilities through this program, I gained valuable firsthand lessons in client interaction and user design that I don't think I could have acquired anywhere else.

At the beginning of the program, before any of us boarded a plane, I learned how important it is to be adaptable. My team and I attempted several times to hold a developer-client meeting, but each one was delayed by unforeseen emergencies and events. We learned early on that no matter how much time and effort we put into planning something, we needed to stay nimble and be ready to think quickly when the situation called for flexibility.

Our first day officially meeting our client was when we visited the Princess Vlei conservation site. My team was informed of the vlei's history, environmental significance, and the myths surrounding the area. After realizing how important the site was to the forum and its community, we understood that whatever we created had to reflect the true essence and significance we experienced there.

As we began development, we gained experience experimenting with different LLM models, since we needed to find one that would not cost our client money they didn't have while still functioning at a level that made the project worthwhile for users. The issues we tackled and had to learn about along the way were scalability, accessibility, and token optimization. After finding the right balance between cost and performance, we settled on a ChatGPT mini LLM as our solution.

I had spent a lot of time since LLMs were first released to the public trying to play with and break them, but testing our own chatbot was the first time I had to test something like this with real intention. I learned different ways to put myself in the mindset of our users, and had to research who those users actually were in order to test properly. Throughout the program, our lectures constantly emphasized that when building software, the target audience has to be the focus, and this was my first real, hands-on encounter with that lesson.

Alongside user-based testing, we also tested edge cases and guardrails. For weeks, a teammate and I were constantly finding and fixing issues as they came up. It felt like a game of whack-a-mole, where fixing one issue only made another appear, and sometimes a new issue turned out to be a variation of one we thought we had already solved. Our professor often told us to test early and test often, and through this process we finally understood exactly what she meant.

The type of testing I had the least experience with was usability testing, since this was the first time I developed software at the direct request of someone who would actually use it themselves, along with their clients. It was a real eye-opening experience. Watching a real user spend several minutes trying to find the icon to our chatbot, even though we thought we had made it obvious, was a clear example of how easily we can misjudge what makes sense to someone outside our own heads. That testing humbled us and taught us to stay open to other people's perspectives so we could keep improving our designs and get the best possible results.

Something that really brought our entire development process into perspective was when our client took us to a local community exhibit to meet the people we were working to help, a community all working together to build each other up along with their neighborhoods and city.

Through this program, I learned many lessons as a software engineer, teammate, developer, and as a person. These lessons are priceless, and I will carry them with me as I move forward in my career.

Nathan Waisserberg — UFIC Study Abroad, Cape Town, South Africa — 2026
